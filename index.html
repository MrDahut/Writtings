Our mission

Most communities have the assets. What they often lack are the connections between them.

Raw resources may lack processing infrastructure. Skilled people may lack access to equipment, training, and markets. Morse Works works on these structural gaps by cultivating practical capacity. We start with the actual problem rather than a predetermined solution, bridge the missing links, and make sure communities keep control and sustain progress long after the project ends.

How we work

Every project starts from its real-world limits. We define the outcome, then build exactly what is needed to bridge the gap.

  1. Define the objective. Set clear outcomes, target users, and success criteria, and cut unnecessary complexity.

  2. Understand conditions. Map local resources, infrastructure, costs, and existing institutional relationships.

  3. Isolate constraints. Identify the technical, operational, or market bottlenecks that are holding progress back.

  4. Develop practical solutions. Use existing local assets, adding or adapting only what is strictly necessary.

  5. Test in the field. Validate assumptions early through prototypes, skill trials, and market pilots under real conditions.

  6. Cultivate lasting capacity. Hand skills, systems, and operational responsibility fully to local leaders and institutions.

Across disciplines

A technical problem often doubles as an organizational or supply-chain barrier, and solving one in isolation usually fails in practice. We analyze problems across domains, design practical pathways forward, and bring in targeted specialists when deeper expertise is needed.

The solution must fit the problem, and the project must fit the place.

An Appalachian network of enterprise

Our long-term aim is a regional network of more than 200 owner-operated and employee-owned enterprises across the State of Franklin region. Inspired by Spain's Mondragon Corporation, we are adapting cooperative principles to Appalachian conditions, workforce traditions, and natural assets.

Skills and capacity

Training and technical know-how shared across member enterprises.

Shared infrastructure

Equipment and facilities that no single small business could carry alone.

Markets and pilots

Joint access to customers, tested before capital is committed.

  • Local ownership. Owner-operated and employee-owned models keep both wealth and decision-making inside the community.
  • Cooperative autonomy. Each enterprise stays independent and collaborates where shared infrastructure or market access creates mutual value.

Current work

We are putting underused assets to work, starting with Appalachian hardwoods.

  • Applied skills development

    Hands-on technical instruction delivered inside real production environments.

  • Production and market piloting

    Testing manufacturing methods, unit economics, customer demand, and distribution channels before committing capital.

  • Resource optimization

    Turning underused local timber into higher-value finished products.

Initiative

Tennessee Hardwood & Carbon (THC)

THC unlocks the economic value of regional timber under the Tennessee Native Species Lumber Act. It connects underused forestry resources with local sawmills, technical workforce training, equipment access, and market development.

Today THC is an internal project of Morse Works. The long-term goal is an independent 501(c)(3) nonprofit dedicated to regional timber industry development.

Operating principles

  • Begin with reality. Work from real ground conditions, not off-the-shelf templates.
  • Build only what is needed. Avoid unnecessary technology, overhead, or complexity.
  • Cultivate independence. Build capacity rather than dependency. Every project should stand on its own.
  • Learn through application. Let practical trials and evidence, not assumptions, drive decisions.
  • Keep people central. Real development lives in the skills and decision-making power of the people who run the systems.
